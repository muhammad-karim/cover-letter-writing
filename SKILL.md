# Cover letters

Source facts: the candidate's master CV. Use only what is in it.

Precedence: the candidate's drafting rules and career coach's advice come first. Their own writing voice guide governs how the words sound. This skill adds structure on top of both.

## 1. What the letter must do

- Say why the candidate wants this job and how they fit it.
- Draw attention to the most important points of the CV. Select and interpret them; do not repeat the CV.
- Stand alone. Assume the reader has not read the CV, so the key evidence sits in the letter too.

## 2. Format

- Exactly one A4 page, usually 300 to 400 words.
- Four paragraphs:
  1. Hook: 2 to 3 sentences on something specific and current about the employer that attracts the candidate, plus a one-line parallel from their career. Use the employer's website, annual report or press, and only what has been verified.
  2. Evidence on the top priority: open the paragraph with that priority's name from the advert, then continue in the candidate's voice with a specific example, its scope and a number.
  3. Evidence on the second priority: same pattern, using the second priority's name.
  4. Close: 1 to 2 forward-looking sentences naming the role and the organisation.
- Each paragraph stays short: no block longer than about 8 lines.
- At least two numbers across the letter, all from the master CV.
- Address any obvious question positively: sector move, seniority, contract history, location.
- If the employer asks for a supporting statement or application form, draft criterion-by-criterion evidence in their order, ready to paste, and say so in the notes.

## 3. Addressing and sign-off

- Address a named person if one can be found. Otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager".
- "Yours sincerely" to a named person, "Yours faithfully" otherwise.
- Sign off with the candidate's name.
- For faith-based organisations, open and close with the greeting that suits the employer.

## 4. Voice

- Plain first person, explaining the point as to a sharp friend. Keep it professional. Simple words over corporate language. Use the candidate's own vocabulary where it fits.
- British spelling.
- No exclamation marks (rare, only for a real moment of surprise). No em-dashes.
- State a strong view plainly and give the evidence before any softening.
- Say how things felt where it helps, plainly. Not detached or only clever.
- Prefer a real, surprising proof point to a polished metaphor. Never invent one.
- Keep sentences short. Use rhythm: a short fragment for emphasis, then a flowing sentence to make the argument.
- Avoid: passionate, thrilled, results-driven, proven track record, dynamic, spearheaded, team player, tidy groups of three adjectives, heavy dashes.
- Paragraphs 2 and 3 open with the skill name from the advert, as the first words of a full sentence in the candidate's voice. Never a bare keyword or a heading-like phrase. Example shape: "Budget ownership is where I've been tested hardest. In my last role I ran a seven-figure budget..." Use only figures from the master CV.

## 5. Honesty

- The master CV is the only source of facts. Titles, employers, dates and figures exactly as stated there.
- "Led", "contributed to" and "part of a team" used accurately. Never claim team results as the candidate's alone.
- Never add or stretch a claim to raise a score.
- Test: would the employer feel misled if they knew the full truth behind this line?
- If an employer asks applicants not to use AI, flag it before drafting and the candidate writes it themselves.

## 6. Checks before it goes out

- Export to PDF. Exactly one page.
- Every number and title checked against the master CV. Employer's name correct throughout.
- File name: "<Name> - Cover letter - <Organisation> - <Role>".
- Note for the candidate: rewrite the hook and close in your own words before sending.
